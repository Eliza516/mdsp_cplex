Abstract
Given a set D of distances, each is a positive integer, the Minimum Distance Superset Problem (MDSP) requires
to retrieve the positions of a set of points on the real line whose pairwise distances make the superset of D. The
problem has applications in X-ray crystallography, and recently plays the key role in enzyme restriction, a technique
to sequence the DNA structure. We propose new upper and lower bounds, and new techniques to reduce the size of
the problem and the number of variables in start-of-the-art Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulations.
In particular, one of our upper bounds is based on a simple, yet efficient large neighborhood search algorithm that
provides very good solutions in short running time. Extensive experiments on randomly generated and real data from
the literature show that our new techniques significantly speed up existing exact methods and close 48 open benchmark
instances. On real instances based on Luciferase, we can solve to optimality 60 over 76 instances.
Keywords: distance supperset, integer programming, enzyme restriction



1. Introduction

    The problem of computing all distances between any pair of n points on a real line is rather simple. However,
the reverse problem of retrieving the position of n points on the line, given their distances is much more challenging.
Formally, the problem can be formulated as following. Given a multiset of k positive integers D = {d1 , d2 , . . . , dk },
we need to define the set P such that D = ∆(P) where ∆(P) = {pi − p j | pi , p j ∈ P, pi < p j }. This problem is known
as Partial Digest Problem (PDP) (12). For example, if D = {1, 1, 2}, then one solution is P = {0, 1, 2} because
∆(P) = {2 − 0, 2 − 1, 1 − 0} = {2, 1, 1}. This problem has been applied in a number of disciplines.
    The first known application of PDP was found in phase retrieval in X-ray crystallography (20; 21), where we
have to retrieve coordinate vectors from the distances vectors of atoms. Because atoms lie in physical space, the
problem is extended to three dimensions, i.e., d ∈ R3 . Another appearance of PDP is the later support in DNA
sequencing (Skiena et al., 1990 (16); Skiena and Sundaram, 1994 (17)). A DNA strand is composed of nucleotides
which have four types A, C, G, T. The DNA sequencing requires determining the order of nucleotides on DNA
chain by taking digestion experiment using restriction enzymes. A restriction enzyme could cut DNA at specific
sequence patterns of nucleotides, called restriction sites, into fragments. For instance, enzyme BamHI cuts DNA at
subsequences GGATCC. By measuring the lengths of the resulting fragments using a standard laboratory technique
called electrophoresis, we can retrieve the original locations of the restriction sites by modeling the problem as an
instance of the PDP.

2. Literature reviews

     Since its birth, the PDP problem has attracted a significant research effort (refer to Fontoura et al. (2018) (10)). In
literature, it has different names. The problem is also called Turnpike problem (Dakic, 2000 (7)), or Chords’ problem
(Daurat et al., 2002 (8), 2005 (9)). If the points lie on a circle, the problem is called Beltway problem (Lemke et al.,
2003 (12)). The PDP problem is a part of a broader subject: the Distance Geometry, which is discussed in an annual
workshop called Distance Geometry Theory and Application (DGTA) (see Alizadeh et al. 2019 (3)). The variants of
and workings related to PDP are summarized in Table 1.
     The hardness of PDP is unknown. Until recently, there is no polynomial algorithm to solve PDP nor a proof to
show that the problem is NP-hard. We only know that in the case the points distribute in a k-dimensional space,
the PDP is proved to be NP-hard (Skiena et al., 1990 (16)). Despite that fact some remarkably efficient algorithms
have proposed. Skiena et al. (1990) (16) published an exact algorithm named backtracking algorithm that archives
a computational complexity of O(2n nlogn) in the worst case, which is shown by Zhang 1994 (22), and in average of
O(n2 logn). However, the Zhang’s instance turns out to be solvable in polynomial time via semi-define programming
algorithm due to Dakic 2000 (7). In the 2016 paper, Abbas et al. (1) improved Skiena’s algorithm with a number of
adding cut - rules for branch and bound strategy and archived better performance on real data. Daurat et al. 2002
(8) proposed two other exact algorithms: one is based on polynomial factorization and another based on solving the

constraints in form of a system of binary quadratic equations, which is called the pyramid algorithm. Another approach
is using heuristic algorithm which is fast but obtains only approximation results (see Dakic 2000 (7), Nadimi et al.
2011 (14) and Ahrabian et al. 2013 (2)).
     PDP has a number of variants. The first version is called Simplified Partial Digest Problem (SPDP) (see Jacek et al.
2011, (11)), also called Labeled Partial Digest Problem (LPDP), where the information about which distances contains
two end points (the leftmost and rightmost points on the line) is given. The distances are classified as interpoint and
end distances. An interpoint distance is the distance between two adjacent points while an end distance is the distance
between the an interpoint and an end point. The problem was triggered by the fact that two ends of a DNA sequence
could be labeled using radioactive-labeling. The main challenge is that the order of points on the sequence is unknown
and the indistinctiveness between two ends. The next problem variant mentioned here is called Probe Partial Digest
Problem (PPDP) which proposed by Karp et al. (13). Before exposing DNA chain to restriction enzyme, we could
mark a location on the chain, e.g. using radical or heat, to get a marked sequence, which is called hybridization probe.
The input data of PPDP is now the lengths of fragments that contain the hybridization probe.

     The ideal case when n2 distances are given correctly is unrealistic. The source of errors is numerous and can
be categorized into four main sources: additional fragments, missing fragments, measurement errors and multiplicity
detection (see Cieliebak et al. 2005 (6) for more details). The imperfection of data leads to erroneous variants of
PDP. The first variant is PDP with additive error bounds and missing a small fraction of fragments was mentioned by
Skiena et al. 1990 (16) and proved to be a strongly NP-hard. Some efficient algorithms to solve erroneous variants
of PDP were proposed such as the generalization of backtracking algorithm which addresses small measurement
errors, inaccurate multiplicity detection or missing a small fraction of fragments. However only recently, two efficient
algorithms based on Mixed Integer programming and Binary search were proposed by Fontoura et al. 2018 (10) which
solve the problems with an arbitrarily high number of missing fragments, which the author called minimum distance
superset problem and briefly written as MDSP. In the paper (10), the authors also provided some mathematical results
about the trivial lower bound and upper bound of MDSP and the congruence of solutions. In this paper, we add a new
visualization of problem with aid of directed graph and provide some more mathematical results that could improve
the proposed algorithms by Fontoura et al. (10).

4. Main Contributions

4.1. Pruning phase
     The aim of the pruning phase is to find the distances that are belonged to a special class that we name the thorn
arcs and then move them away from the distances multiset to offload the difficulty of the problem. A thorn arc distance
(if there exists) is the distance whose corresponding arc is not included in any simple cycle in any visual graph of the
any distance superset. Following Theorem 1, we could safely remove the thorn arc distances to reduce the size of the
problem.
     For example, with D = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 100}, d = 100 is a thorn arc distance. The reason is that 100 is too
large comparing to the other elements of D so that every linear combination including 100 and some other elements
of D with coefficients ±1 is nonzero.
     We give a formal definition of thorn arcs: A distance d ∈ D is thorn arc if there does not exist elements of D \ {d}
whose linear combination with coefficients ±1 equals to d. One property of the thorn arc is that: if d is the thorn arc
of D, P∗D\{d} ∪ {p + d} is an minimum distance super-set of D where P∗D\{d} is an minimum distance superset of D \ {d}
and p ∈ P∗D\{d} . We generalize that property in the following theorem which is the main result of this subsection.
Theorem 1. Let Dh be the set of thorn arcs of D. If P∗h is the minimum distance superset of D \ Dh , we have
P∗ = P∗h ∪ (Dh + p) is also an optimal solution of D, where p ∈ P∗h .
     Theorem 1 suggests that we could remove the thorn arcs from the distance set D to reduce the problem size.
     An approach to detect the thorn arcs is applying the Subset sum problem. Given the integers w1 , w2 , . . . , wn , the
Subset sum problem determines if there is any subset of them that sum to preciously another integer W (see Tomohiro
and Akihiro, 2016 (18) for more information). If d ∈ D is a thorn arc, there exists a subset of (D \ {d}) ∪ −(D \ {d})
of which sum of elements equals to d, where −(D \ {d}) is the set of additive inverse value of elements of (D \ {d}).
Although Subset sum problem is a NP complete problem (18), the following dynamic algorithm could help to solve it
efficiently in case the number and values of integers are not large.

4.2. Lower bound based on transforming MDSP on Z to MDSP on ring Z/gZ
     The improvement on lower bound helps to reduce the symmetry of search space of IP model. More precisely, via
constraints (13) we could eliminates l variables z1 , z2 , . . . , zl by fixing their values to 1. On the other hand, the number
of constraints (10) would also decrease by l constraints. In addition, this is also helpful for binary search models. The
initial search range of binary search is from l to u, therefore improvement on lower bound l helps to narrow this search
range.
     To improve lower bound, we transform the MDSP on integer set Z into MDSP on ring of integers modulo g:
Z/gZ = {0, 1, . . . , g − 1}. The distance modulo g between points p, q ∈ Z/gZ is defined as following: dg (p, q) =
min{|p − q|, g − |p − q|}, e.g. for g = 5, d5 (1, 4) = min{|1 − 4|, 5 − |1 − 4|} = 2. D is then mapped into the distance
multiset modulo g: Dg = {min{d mod g, g − (d mod g)}|d ∈ D}. The modulo g of distance superset is denoted as
Pg . The problem Minimum Distance Superset on Z/gZ is denoted as MDSPg .
     Let consider an example of MDSPg on distance multiset D = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} and g = 3. The distance multiset
modulo g = 3 is D3 = {1, 1, 0, 1, 1}. Back to general case of g, we denote xi , for 0 ≤ i ≤ g − 1, as the number of
points equal to i in Pg , as a sequence we have x0 + . . . + xg−1 = |Pg |. We denote m j as the number of distances whose
value is equal to j in Dg , for 0 ≤ j ≤ g/2. It is clear that xi points equal to i and xi+ j points equal to i + j will generate
xi xi+ j distances equal to j. Hence, the superset constraints are followings inequalities,

4.4. Large neighborhood search for MDSP
    In this subsection, we introduce another algorithm that also aims to improve upper bound for MDSP. Via construct
and destroy process, we receive a distance superset P and then set the cardinality of this superset as an upper bound.
The main idea of construction phase is to consider every integer on [0, B] as potential candidate to be added to P. For
each integer, we compute the differences between it and all elements of P and count the number of differences which
belong to D. The integer whose most number of differences belong to D will be added to P. This construction stops
when ∆(P) fully covers D. After that the destruction is executed by removing a number of elements of P and then the
construction is repeated again. The process of construction-destruction is repeated in a certain times. The following
step-by-step describes the algorithm.
    Step 1. This step aims to construct a distance superset P. Before coming in details, let make clear about some
denotation. Let D′ denote the set containing uncovered distances by P, i.e. D′ = D \ ∆(P). We write the set of
distances between integer x and elements of P that belong to D′ as nd(P, D′ , x) = {x − a | a ∈ P} ∩ D′ . This step
starts with a nonempty set P. At the first time execution Step 1, P is assigned to {0} but at the next time, P is assigned
to the output of Step 2. We gradually build point set P by consider integers in [0, B]. For each integer x, we compute
|nd(P, D′ , x)|, then add the integer whose the largest |nd(P, D′ , x)| to P. If there are more than one integers x whose the
largest |nd(P, D′ , x)|, we choose one integer randomly. Then we update P and D′ : D′ = D′ \ nd(P, D′ , x), P = P ∪ {x}.
    The Step 1 is executed until D′ is empty. Cause of its essence, this step is called Construction Step.
    Step 2. This step is named Destruction Step. The output of this step is a subset Q ⊂ P which contains b elements
of P, where b = ⌊0.1 × |P|⌋. This step is almost the same as Step 1 except two points: first, we consider only elements
of P not the integers between 0 and B; second, we execute this step until b elements are added to Q. At the beginning,
Q is set to {0} and D′ is set to D. In the processing of Step 2, element x ∈ P whose the largest |nd(P, D′ , x)| will be
added to Q. After x is added to Q, we update D′ , P: D′ = D \ nd(P \ {x}, x, D′ ), P = P \ {x}.
    We then repeat Step 1. The output Q of Step 2 becomes the input set P of Step 1. We repeat steps 1-2 for t times,
which in experiments is set to t = 100. The smallest P after t times is the output of this algorithm.
    Step 1 takes O(B(|D|+1)2 ) in the worst case while Step 2 takes O(B|D|) in the worst case. Therefore, the complexity
of this greedy algorithm is O(tB|D|2 ). The detail is described in Algorithm 4.

6. Conclusions

    In this paper, we show some mathematical results relating graph modelling, and then we prove a value upper
bound for some optimal solutions and develop a method to reduce problem size by detecting thorn arc. Our new
bounds allow to close the gap for Fontoura et al.’s data, solving more instances for all classes of instances.
    For the future work, we recommend to discovery new mathematical properties of MDSP problem. A promising
approach is to study the problem in another space V, e.g. the group modulo g as proposed in this paper. The new
space V would have the following properties: (i) reserving the difference, i.e. if x − y = d then ϕ(x) − ϕ(y) = ϕ(d),
where ϕ is the map function from the integer set Z to V (ii) the new MDSP problem on V is easier to solve. We do
believe that the key to obtain more fruitful results is Arithmetic Combinatorics, a recent rapidly developing subject
of Mathematics. The nature essence of MDSP suggest many relations to Arithmetic Combinatorics, e.g. the sumset
operation and arithmetic properties of solution.
    The Erroneous MDSP variant (EMDSP) could be an interesting research direction. One simple case is that some
distances may flutters around some values, e.g. we could accept a solution that does not exactly cover the distance d
but an approximation value in range [d − αd , d + βd ]. To deal with this error, then the constraints 2, 3 could be supplant
by following inequalities with αd = βd = 1,

                                 pi + xidj × (d − αd ) ≤ p j , 1 ≤ i < j ≤ u, d ∈ Dh ,                                      (30)
                                 p j ≤ pi + M × (1 − xidj ) + dh + βd , 1 ≤ i < j ≤ u, d ∈ Dh .                             (31)

     This also suggests a new approach to find a better lower bound, i.e., we could reduce size of MDSP problem by
dividing all distance to a certain divisor h: D = {dh = ⌊d/h⌋ | d ∈ D}. We have ⌊(x−y)/h⌋ ≤ ⌊x/h⌋−⌊y/h⌋ ≤ (x−y)/h⌋+1,
for every x ≥ y ≥ 0. Therefore, the MDSP is then transformed into the EMDSP with αd = 0 and βd = 1. If we take
D = {dh = ⌈d/h⌉ | d ∈ D}, then analogously we could retrieve an EMDSP with αd = 1 and βd = 0. The solution of
EMDSP is the lower bound for the MDSP.
     A sophisticated strategy to improve the maximal limitation B for points in P, as suggested in the end of Subsection

if-then algorithm.
     To conclude, we discuss now the symmetry of the search space. The final phase to solve MSDP is different search
algorithms. If we could reduce the symmetry of search space, we bring down the size the search space, hence we
